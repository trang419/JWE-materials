Growth accounting: the framework

Economic growth comes from two different sources:

  • More inputs: working more hours, employing more people, building more capital, or having a more educated workforce
  • Using inputs better: technology, efficiency, and innovation that let the same inputs produce more output (this is called Total Factor Productivity, or TFP)
  • Frame the upcoming charts: each panel shows the annual growth rate (%) of one input, or of TFP
  • Preview: Japan’s inputs kept growing, but TFP growth stalled — that’s the story the next few slides build toward
  • Optional if asked: TFP is measured as a residual — growth left over after accounting for capital, labor, and human capital growth

Growth accounting: Japan

  • Growth drivers: capital, total factor productivity, human capital: investment-led

Role of currency

  • Store of value: held as reserves by central banks and investors
  • Medium of exchange: used to invoice and settle cross-border trade, even between two other countries
  • Unit of account: used to price global assets like oil, or benchmark other currencies against

Yen as store of value

  • Frame the upcoming chart: it shows only one dimension (store of value / reserve holdings), not trade invoicing or pricing, worth noting the yen may play a bigger or smaller role in other functions
  • Preview: the yen has held a stable, mid-sized share of global reserves for decades — this reflects Japan’s role as a safe-haven currency despite its shrinking share of world GDP
  • Optional if asked: contrast with the US dollar, which dominates in all three functions simultaneously, that’s part of what “reserve currency” usually implies beyond just what this chart shows

Why demographics matter

  • Growth drivers: labor supply directly feeds into the growth accounting framework

  • Market size: a country’s population sets the scale of domestic demand

  • Consumption: a population’s age structure drives spending patterns

  • Saving: national saving rates (working-age people save for retirement; retirees draw savings down)

  • Callback: ties directly to the growth-accounting slide — labor was one of the four inputs; this section asks “what happens when that input starts shrinking?”
  • Preview: Japan’s median age is now the highest among peers, with population decline already underway — the next two slides show this directly, plus whether migration is offsetting it
  • Optional if asked: aging affects saving in offsetting ways — life-cycle theory predicts lower aggregate saving as retirees dissave, but this is debated empirically for Japan specifically (household saving rates have been unusually resilient)

Japan at a glance: takeaways

  • High-income: Japan converged with advanced economies decades ago, but stalled since the 1990s
  • Growth model: Initially investment- and export-led, with rising TFP but since the 1990s, all drivers slowed
  • Safe-haven currency: the yen has held a stable share of global reserves for decades
  • Aging economy: a shrinking, aging population is now squeezing the labor input directly
  • Japan’s trajectory as a cautionary tale or a preview of what other aging economies (Korea, China, parts of Europe) will face
